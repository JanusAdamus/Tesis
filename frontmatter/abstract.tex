This thesis studies what different probabilistic sequence models can show when a symbolic melody is represented as an ordered sequence of pitch classes. The development begins with finite Markov chains. Hidden Markov models (HMMs) then separate the observed notes from latent musical contexts, while an HMM based on the hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP-HMM) avoids assuming that the number of latent states is known beforehand. Computational experiments accompany this theoretical development, and their limits of reproducibility and interpretation are made explicit.

The final experiment evaluates a finite HMM, a truncated HDP-HMM, a variable-order Markov model, and a causal transformer on the same task of predicting the next event. The data are split by canonical work, keeping arrangements of the same piece together. Of the 3,000 sampled PDMX files, 2,933 pieces remained after preprocessing.

At the full training fraction, the transformer obtained a test perplexity of 5.823. The corresponding values in the primary run were 7.356 for the finite HMM, 7.968 for the HDP-HMM, and 8.022 for the variable-order model. Four audited sensitivity runs varied window overlap, finite-HMM capacity, and the data split one factor at a time. The work-level paired ranking placed the transformer first in every run, although removing window overlap reduced its margin over the finite HMM, and expanding the finite-HMM grid improved that model to a perplexity of 6.984 without locating its optimum. These results support a predictive advantage at the corpus scale studied, but do not establish structural superiority. The solution was technologically viable as a research prototype on the equipment used; its economic convenience depends on the relative CPU and GPU rates, and its social viability was not evaluated with users. The experiment also lacked reference annotations for phrases, sections, or harmonic functions, so these limitations remain distinct from the predictive result.
