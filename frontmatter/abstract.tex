This thesis studies what different probabilistic sequence models can show when a symbolic melody is represented as an ordered sequence of pitch classes. The development begins with finite Markov chains. Hidden Markov models (HMMs) then separate the observed notes from latent musical contexts, while an HMM based on the hierarchical Dirichlet process (HDP-HMM) avoids assuming that the number of latent states is known beforehand. Computational experiments accompany this theoretical development, and their limits of reproducibility and interpretation are made explicit.

The final experiment evaluates a finite HMM, a truncated HDP-HMM, a variable-order Markov model, and a causal transformer on the same task of predicting the next event. The data are split by canonical work, keeping arrangements of the same piece together. Of the 3,000 sampled PDMX files, 2,933 pieces remained after preprocessing.

At the full training fraction, the transformer obtained a test perplexity of 5.823. The corresponding values were 7.356 for the finite HMM, 7.968 for the HDP-HMM, and 8.022 for the variable-order model. These results favor the transformer for prediction at the scale studied, but they do not establish structural superiority. The completed run had no reference annotations for phrases, sections, or harmonic functions, so the predictive result is not taken as evidence of musical interpretation. Prediction, structural correspondence, latent-state compactness, and computational cost remain separate criteria rather than interchangeable measures.
